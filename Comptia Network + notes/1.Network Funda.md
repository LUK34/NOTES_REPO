# 1. Networking fundamentals

### Types of Networks:
- **1. LAN (Local Area Network)**
- Small area network (like home, office, school).
- Covers a single building or floor.
- **Example:** WiFi at home, office computers connected to a switch.

- **2. MAN (Metropolitan Area Network)**
- Medium area network.
- Covers a city or large campus.
- **Example:** A city-wide internet service provider (ISP) network.

- **3. WAN (Wide Area Network)**
- Large area network.
- Covers countries or the whole world.
- **Example:** The Internet is the biggest WAN.

- **4. CAN (Campus Area Network)**
- Network covering a campus (university, hospital, or military base).
- Bigger than LAN but smaller than MAN.
- **Example:** University connecting all departments and hostels.

- **5. SAN (Storage Area Network)**
- Special network that connects servers to storage devices (like big hard drives).
- Used in data centers and hospitals for fast storage access.
- **Example:** A hospital’s database system storing patient records on a SAN.

- **6. PAN (Personal Area Network)**
- Smallest type of network.
- Covers only a few meters around a person.
- Used to connect personal devices like phones, laptops, smartwatches, earbuds, etc.
- **Example:**
- Bluetooth between your phone and earbuds.
- Hotspot from your mobile to your laptop.

- **High level overview:**
- PAN → LAN → CAN → MAN → WAN

### Network Switch and Backbone
- **1. Switch**
- A switch is a network device that connects computers, servers, printers, or other devices within a Local Area Network (LAN).
- It operates mainly at Layer 2 (Data Link Layer) of the OSI model (but some advanced switches also work at Layer 3).
- **Function:** It forwards data only to the intended device (MAC address) instead of broadcasting to all devices like a hub.
- **Example:** If PC1 sends data to PC2, the switch looks at PC2’s MAC address and forwards it directly.

- **2. Backbone**
- A backbone is the main central pathway in a network that carries the bulk of traffic.
- Think of it like the highway of a network that connects different smaller roads (LANs).

- Example: In a hospital or university, each building might have its own LAN,
- and the backbone connects all these LANs together at high speed (using fiber optics usually).

- **3. Segment**
- A segment is a section of a network, often separated for performance or security reasons.
- In older terms, a segment was the portion of a network where all devices shared the same collision domain (like with hubs).
- With switches today, segments usually mean:
- **LAN segment: ** a group of devices connected to one switch or part of a switch.
- **Subnet segment: ** a group of IPs within the same subnet.

- **4. VLAN (Virtual Local Area Network)**
- A VLAN is a logical grouping of devices within a switch (or multiple switches), regardless of their physical location.
- **Purpose:** It separates network traffic for security and efficiency.
```
Example: In a hospital:

VLAN 10 → Doctors
VLAN 20 → Nurses
VLAN 30 → Admin staff
```
- Even if their computers are plugged into the same switch, VLANs keep their network traffic isolated unless a router/firewall is used.

- **Quick Analogy:**
- **Switch:** Smart traffic cop inside one neighborhood.
- **Backbone:** The main highway connecting neighborhoods.
- **Segment:** A smaller street or block inside a neighborhood.
- **VLAN:** A gated community inside the neighborhood, only members allowed.

- **High Level Overview:**
- Backbone (core) → connects to Switches (distribution/access) → which serve Segments (end devices/users)


### Network topologies
- **1. Bus Topology**
- All devices share one central cable (the “bus”).
- Cheap, easy to set up, but if cable fails → whole network down.
- **Example:** Early Ethernet networks.


- **2. Star Topology**
- All devices connect to a central switch/hub.
- Easy to manage, one device fails → others still work.
- Most common in LANs today.

- **3. Ring Topology**
- Devices connected in a circle.
- Data travels one way (or both in dual-ring).
- If one device/cable fails → can break the network.
- **Example:** Token Ring networks.

- **4. Mesh Topology**
- Every device connects to every other device.
- Very reliable (many paths), but expensive.
- **Example:** Used in backbone networks, military, wireless mesh WiFi.

- **5. Tree (Hierarchical) Topology**
- Combination of star + bus.
- Devices grouped in stars, then connected to a main bus backbone.
- **Example:** Large corporate networks.

- **6. Hybrid Topology**
- Mix of two or more topologies (like star + ring, star + mesh).
- Flexible and scalable.
- **Example:** Modern data centers.

- **Quick Summary (Analogy):**
- Bus → One straight road, all houses along it.
- Star → Houses connected to one junction.
- Ring → Houses in a circle road.
- Mesh → Every house connected to every other house.
- Tree → Many stars connected like branches of a tree.
- Hybrid → A mix of different layouts.



### Cisco 3-layer Hierarchial Network model
- **1. Core Layer (Backbone)**
- The fast, high-speed backbone of the network.
- **Purpose:** Transport data quickly across the network.
- **Devices:** High-performance routers, Layer-3 switches.
- **Characteristics:** High speed, reliability, low latency.
- **Example:** In a university, the core connects all campus buildings together.

- **2. Distribution Layer (sometimes called Aggregation/Data Layer)**
- The middle layer between core and access.
- **Purpose:** Controls and manages traffic.
- **Functions:** Routing, filtering, policy enforcement (like VLAN routing, firewalls, ACLs).
- **Devices:** Layer-3 switches, routers.
- **Example:** Decides whether traffic from one department can reach another.


- **3. Access Layer**
- Closest to end devices (users, PCs, printers, phones, IoT devices).
- **Purpose:** Provides access to the network.
- **Devices:** Switches, wireless access points.
- **Example:** Your laptop or printer plugs into an access switch.


### Spine and Leaf Architecture
- Mostly used in data centers.
- Every Leaf switch connects to all Spine switches.
- There is no direct Leaf-to-Leaf or Spine-to-Spine connection.

-**Leaf Switches**
- At the Access layer (connect to servers, routers, firewalls).
- Provide endpoints with network access.

-**Spine Switches**
- At the Core layer (high-speed backbone).
- Provide fast connectivity between all leaf switches.

- **Advantages:**
- Predictable low latency.
- High bandwidth (lots of equal paths).
- Scales easily → just add more spine/leaf switches.

- **Analogy:**
- Leaf switches = buildings in a city.
- Spine switches = highways connecting all buildings.
- Everyone can reach everyone else quickly.


### Collapsed Core Architecture
- Simplified version of the 3-layer model.
- Combines Core layer and Distribution layer into one layer.
- Often used in smaller networks (like campuses or medium enterprises).

- **Access Layer** → Users connect here.
- **Collapsed Core** → Provides both backbone speed and policy control (routing, filtering, VLANs) in one device.

- **Advantages:**
- Cheaper (fewer devices needed).
- Easier to manage.
- Still provides redundancy if done correctly.

- **Analogy:**
- Instead of having highways + city main roads, you just have one big road that does both jobs.

### Traffic flow direction
- **North–South Traffic**
- Traffic that enters or leaves the data center / network.
- Flows between users (clients) and servers.
- **Direction:** Up and down (north ↕ south).
- **Example:**
- A doctor in a hospital opens the HIS portal → request goes from user’s PC (south) to the application server in data center (north).
- A user browsing a website → traffic from laptop → core network → internet.

- **East–West Traffic**
- Traffic that stays inside the data center / network.
- Flows between servers, VMs, or microservices.
- **Direction:** Side to side (east ↔ west).
- **Example:**
- HIS application server ↔ Database server.
- Web server ↔ API server ↔ Storage server inside the data center.


### Network traffic
- **Unicast:**
- Unicast is a one-to-one form of communication where data is sent from one source to one specific destination identified by a unique IP address.
- It is the most common form of IP communication, used for most internet traffic, including web browsing, email, and file transfers.
- Unicast communication ensures that data packets are delivered to a single, specific recipient over a network.

- **Multicast:**
- Multicast is a method of communication where data is sent from one or more sources to multiple destinations simultaneously over a network, using a specific multicast group address.
- Multicast is efficient for applications like streaming video or audio, where the same data needs to be delivered to multiple recipients,
- reducing the bandwidth consumption compared to sending separate copies of the data to each recipient.
- This approach is used in both IPv4 and IPv6 networks to optimize the delivery of packets to multiple destinations.

- **Anycast:**
- Anycast is a network addressing and routing method where data is sent to the nearest or best destination as determined by routing
- protocols, from among multiple potential destinations sharing the same address. It is used in IPv6 (and to a lesser extent in IPv4)
- to provide fast and efficient delivery of services by directing users to the closest server, commonly used in DNS and CDN (Content Delivery Network) services.
- Anycast can improve network performance and availability by automatically routing requests to the nearest data center.

- **Broadcast:**
- Broadcast is a communication method where a message is sent from one sender to all potential receivers within a network segment.
- In IPv4, the broadcast address is used to send data to all devices on a LAN simultaneously, such as when a device requests an IP address via DHCP.
- Broadcast is not supported in IPv6; instead, multicast addresses are used for similar purposes.
